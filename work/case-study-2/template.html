Why monitoring?

Of everything that could make the product more useful,
three signals put monitoring at the top of the list.

01
Email is the strongest retention lever. It carries the update history, and once users open the dashboard through it, we could confirm the need for monitoring.
02
Users want to know what's helping or hurting their credit score. We've already started working on the 1st and 2nd features, so we decided to build the 3rd.
03
In interviews, users asked for an active alert system and monitoring whenever their score changes or any data updates.
3Problem
"Your score changed" brings users back, then the dashboard goes silent.
Users return to the dashboard because an email told them their score moved. But the dashboard gives no indication of what changed, where, or why.

Competitor Analysis

I looked at how other products handle monitoring, where it lives, and how they surface what moved a score.

Goal
See how monitoring, which tells users what factors made their score drop or rise, is presented and where it sits in the layout.
Apps researched
  • Monzo
  • Capitalise
  • Experian
Key insights
01
Monitoring is placed close to the main score.
02
They don't show every factor at once, they move you to a dedicated screen to see the full list.
03
Alerts are split into categories by priority (negative, positive, neutral).
Key takeaway
Across services, monitoring followed a similar UI pattern: main score, then monitoring, then action.
→ Next step: decide what information this section should show, and in which categories and priority.

6Solutions
Quick Workshop

To get past my own bias toward the current dashboard, I brought in other people's eyes and ran a short workshop to explore layouts from scratch.

Goal
  • Find common patterns across participants' layouts and apply them to the design.
  • Get a fresh perspective, free of my bias.
Constraint
The business credit score, sub scores, cross-sell, and action & monitoring all had to be there.
Each participant played the user, deciding which information they'd want to see, in what order, and where, then shared their rationale.
What we learned
01Everyone wants to find the business credit score first. Every single layout led with it.
02People wanted the action for increasing the main score to be the first thing they see.
03They wanted update alerts to sit right next to the main or sub scores, so it's intuitive where a change happened.
Key takeaway
  • The workshop let me structure the information with a fresh perspective.
  • Participants' patterns weren't far apart, but they differed from the current dashboard, so I quickly committed to a redesign.
→ Next step: move beyond the existing dashboard and redesign it, monitoring included, drawing on user and workshop insights.

7Usability Testing

The dashboard is the main screen of the service, so before launch I ran usability testing for one last look at whether anything in the redesign got in users’ way.

Goal
  • Check whether users understand what each piece of information is.
  • Check whether they use the product in the intended pattern.
  • Look for any other problems and friction points.
Participants
10 desktop users · 10 mobile users
1Hypotheses
  • Users understand each piece of information presented on the dashboard.
  • Users understand what changed and affected their score and why it happened.
  • Users understand that the overall score is made up of the four sub-sections.
  • Users navigate the product in order: dashboard → updates → actions → four sub-sections → loan
  • Users understand each of the four sub-sections by clicking into them and reading the detail descriptions.
2Findings
When collapsed, the update count gets confused with the other numbers on screen
On mobile the update was collapsed by default. A user read the count of 6 against the sub scores and loan banner below it, and could not work out why it said 6. Only after opening the update did they understand what the 6 was counting.
"It says 6 up here, but there are only 5 below. Why 6?"
Takeaway: collapsing the update by default makes the count confusing.
